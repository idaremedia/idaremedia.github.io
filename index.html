Case study 01 · Hearts of Iron IV · Summer Sale 2026

"I Got Reincarnated as a Field Marshal and Now I'm Stuck on a Beach"

A routine summer-sale brief became an original four-episode series and two specials, with characters the community adopted as its own meme.

My rolePitch, script, visual approach, production, publishing
FormatVisual-novel style shorts, 4 episodes
ConstraintA 20-hour week, alongside the regular trailer schedule
ResultOne episode became four, plus two specials
01 Brief

The Summer Sale brief asked for two talking generals with basic South Park style animation and summer vibes.

02 Idea

I am a huge fan of the Yakuza games, where the story pauses and the hero spends an afternoon on something gloriously ridiculous. So I dressed MacArthur and Montgomery in Hawaiian shirts and gave them silly dialogue, using voice-over lines from the game. Strategy players spend hundreds of hours on serious campaigns, then share the most absurd things that happened in them, and the series told exactly those stories.

03 Production

The script came first, built around the El Alamein joke Montgomery simply cannot stop making. Then I found the right background and the right poses for the generals, and only at the very end the music that sounds like a summer substory.

That is the reverse of my usual habit, where I find the soundtrack first and plan every scene around it. One rule stayed the same: the editor stays closed until the video already exists in my head, which is what makes a full production fit into half a working week.

04 Result

Management requested three more episodes. The community turned Montgomery's obsession with El Alamein into a meme, and in the fourth episode, where I left the joke out, viewers quoted it in the comments themselves. Players even started asking for a Hearts of Iron IV visual-novel dating sim. After the series, MacArthur and Montgomery came back in two more specials: the Classic Sale and HOIloween.

05 Lesson

A good meme belongs to the audience long before it belongs to its author. Episodes that open on a moment players have lived land faster than ones that need context, so every brief since starts with a recognisable first three seconds.

Case study 02 · Hearts of Iron IV · Anniversary Sale

Finding the hook in a ten-year-old trailer

The best opening I ever made was not mine. It was ten years old, and I found it only after I stopped looking at my own timeline and started looking backwards.

My roleConcept, edit, delivery
FormatSale short for YouTube and TikTok
ConstraintFixed sale date, existing footage only
ResultBeat the channel's previous engagement record
01 Brief

A short for the April Anniversary Sale. I had everything a sale video usually needs: animated DLC footage, cuts from the official trailers and gameplay that shows what makes the game what it is.

02 Problem

On paper it worked, but the first seconds explained the game instead of making anyone feel something, and a scroller would not stop for it.

03 What I did

I watched every official Hearts of Iron IV trailer, back to the announcement a decade ago. The very first one ran real historical footage under a Churchill speech. I kept only the speech and let it build the tension for the first seconds, so the voice carried the weight and my images could follow it instead of fighting for attention.

04 Result

It became the strongest piece of engagement on the Hearts of Iron IV channel that I have worked on and beat the channel's previous record. The opening was so serious that part of the audience was sure they were watching the announcement of Hearts of Iron V: a compliment and a warning in the same comment section.

05 Lesson

The archive of the brand you work for is often richer than anything new you can invent. And test your first two seconds by asking what a stranger will believe they are about to watch, because every hook makes a promise.

Case study 03 · Victoria 3 · TikTok

Launching a TikTok channel from zero

The hardest part was not the editing or the algorithm, but the silence of a channel with zero followers, where every video feels like a speech to an empty hall.

My roleHelped launch the channel, content production
FormatEngagement, sale and announcement shorts
ConstraintMost posts had a job to do: a sale or an announcement
ResultAbout 400 followers in five months
01 Start

The very first video was not an advert but an engagement piece, made simply to say hello to the players.

02 Rhythm

After it the channel found its rhythm in sale videos with an entertaining twist and in announcements of every kind, including the open beta. The art is making a post with a job feel like something worth watching rather than something to scroll past.

03 What worked

Trains. For Steam's Train Fest we made a piece about the railways of Victoria 3, and it brought more engagement than anything else on the channel.

04 What I would change

The balance. Pure engagement content for Victoria 3 only appeared together with a sale, while Hearts of Iron IV had its own series. I would give Victoria 3 the same focus, because that is exactly where the channel started to talk with players instead of at them.

05 Lesson

A game about economies and politics becomes exciting in thirty seconds the moment you find the thing its players already love talking about, and then simply let them talk.

Case study 04 · ClawBerry Workshop · Etsy

Launching a family jewelry shop on Etsy from zero

My wife makes one-of-a-kind beaded jewelry. I built the shop around her pieces: the listings, the photos and video, the search strategy and the plan for Instagram and Pinterest.

My roleShop setup, listings, photo and video, marketing plan
FormatEtsy shop, Instagram and Pinterest
ConstraintEvery piece is unique, so each listing sells only once
Result13 listings live in the first month, the tested ones ranking first for their search
01 Brief

A small studio run from our apartment in Madrid. Zlata designs and strings every piece by hand, pairing freshwater pearls and natural stones with tiny fruit charms. I handle the photos, listings and messages, and our cat Marusya is the quality inspector.

02 Problem

A unique piece sells once, so no listing can grow reviews over time. Research also showed that fruit-charm jewelry is a small niche: raspberry and fruit terms have almost no searches on Etsy.

03 What I did

I completed the eRank course "Prepare for the holidays" and applied every lesson to the shop on the same day. I researched holiday keywords, compared 17 competitor prices across three shops, gave each listing one superstar keyword in its title, tags and description, and set Christmas order-by dates with a holiday banner and an order-by card.

Traffic now aims at pearl and gemstone searches, while raspberry stays the brand word. Around the listings I produce the content: a sunlit reel cut from phone footage, from raw pearl strands to finished pieces, and a mystical collection reel made with AI video.

04 Result

Thirteen listings went live in the first month, across bracelets, necklaces and sets. An Etsy search for "raspberry charm pearl bracelet" shows all three tested listings first. The strongest keyword I found, "baroque pearl necklace", has over 1,000 monthly searches and the lowest difficulty of the set. The first sales are the next milestone.

05 Lesson

When every product is one of a kind, the brand is what people come back for, because the piece they loved is already gone. So the shop, the reels and the cat have to be memorable on their own.

Research · El Tardeo by Madrid in Game · 25 November 2026

How game launches changed: from the magazine cover to the viral clip

A conversation between PR consultant Teresa Núñez, who has worked with publishers such as Bethesda and Capcom, and Daniel Acal, former director of PlayMania, at the Campus del Videojuego in Madrid. Written up from my notes of the session.

Five conclusions

  1. The gatekeepers changed. Launches once depended on previews, magazine covers and trade shows. Today many publishers skip hands-on previews, release trailers and go straight to reviews, while a viral short can shape opinion faster than a review.
  2. Press and creators are not enemies. Both speakers agreed there is room for traditional media, YouTubers, streamers and short-form creators at the same time.
  3. PR is skilled work that is often underestimated. The press release is the visible end of preparation, timing, material and follow-up.
  4. Respect for the journalist's time is the core of good PR. Strong material early, and follow-ups that add something.
  5. Personal relationships still matter, especially in Spain. The personal touch with journalists and local talent remains a strength of the Spanish market.

What it means for launch and content production

  • Treat the trailer as the start of the campaign, not the end. With fewer previews, the trailer sets expectations, so plan what it promises and what follows it.
  • Plan press and creators together. Decide early which material goes to journalists, which to creators, and in what order.
  • Build material early. Late assets cost relationships with media and creators.
  • Make every follow-up useful: a new asset, a date, an answer.
  • Use shared remote sessions. A live play session with a moderator can reach many outlets at once, fairly.
  • Keep a local, personal layer. A known contact still opens doors that data alone does not.

Statements are paraphrased from my own notes, not quoted. Background: E3 was last held in person in 2019 and was ended permanently by the Entertainment Software Association in December 2023.

At El Tardeo by Madrid in Game, Teresa Núñez and Daniel Acal talked about the launches that changed our industry, and what stayed with me was not the nostalgia for E3 corridors and magazine covers, but the quiet admission that where a launch once lived or died by previews and the cover of a magazine, today a single viral short can weigh more than a review, and yet both of them agreed that the press, the creators and the studios still need each other.

Thank you to Madrid in Game and to both speakers for an evening that felt less like a history lesson and more like a map of where launches are going next.

Career

How I am spending the months before March

Almost a year ago I joined Paradox Interactive as a part-time Social Media Content Creator, and somewhere between the sale trailers, the community videos and a Victoria 3 TikTok channel that started from zero, I realised that the part of the work I love most has a name, and that name is creative producer.

Read the full postShow less

Now that my contract comes to an end in March, I have decided to use the months in between as well as I can, rather than wait for the last week and panic like a student before an exam.

The first part is the work itself. There are still sale trailers on my desk and community pieces for Hearts of Iron IV and Victoria 3 that deserve the same care as the very first ones, and I would rather leave behind a few videos the community remembers than a list of things I meant to finish. Paradox gave me my first chance to work inside a games company, after years of playing their games since Crusader Kings II, and the best thank you I know is to do every remaining piece well.

The second part is the search, and here I try to be slow on purpose. Instead of sending a hundred identical applications, I talk to producers, ask how their teams really work, what a good first month looks like and which mistakes they see when someone moves from content into production, and only then do I apply, with a CV and a letter written for that team and nobody else. It means fewer applications than a spreadsheet would like, although my spreadsheet, I must admit, is very demanding XD

The third part is the uncomfortable one, and it is simply not knowing: not knowing which conversation will turn into something, which silence is a no and which is just a busy recruiter, and learning to keep making good work while the most important answer of the year has not arrived yet.

So I would love to hear from anyone who has been here: what helped you most between roles, or between industries?

A speech given to an empty hall: launching a TikTok channel from zero

When I helped launch the Victoria 3 TikTok channel, the hardest part was not the editing, the scripts or even the algorithm, but the strange silence of a channel with zero followers, where every video you post feels like a speech given to an empty hall, and you still have to give it as if the hall were full.

Read the full postShow less

The very first video was not an advert but an engagement piece, made simply to say hello to the players, and after it the channel found its rhythm in sale videos with an entertaining twist and in announcements of every kind, which is the honest reality of a game channel inside a company: most of what you post has a job to do, and the art is in making that job feel like something worth watching rather than something you have to scroll past.

The video that did best of all was, of course, about trains. For Steam's Train Fest we made a piece about the railways of Victoria 3, and it brought more engagement than anything else on the channel, which taught me that a grand strategy game about economies, politics and pops becomes exciting in thirty seconds the moment you find the one thing its players already love talking about, and then simply let them talk.

The one thing I would change is the balance, because pure engagement content for Victoria 3 only appeared when it travelled together with a sale, while Hearts of Iron IV had its own series and its own space to be playful, and looking back I would give Victoria 3 the same focus and many more videos made just for its community, since that is exactly where the channel started to talk with players instead of at them.

Five months later the channel has around 400 followers, which is not a number anyone would put on a billboard, but it is 400 people who chose a game about nineteenth-century politics in the middle of their feed, and I learned more from building those first hundreds than from any video that goes viral by accident.

So I am curious: if someone handed you an empty channel for a game tomorrow, what would your first ten videos be?

Game analysis · Carousel

The first hour of a game is itself a trailer: Zenless Zone Zero

In 2023 I took part in the closed beta of Zenless Zone Zero, well before it reached the rest of the world, and what stayed with me was not a single fight or a single character but the quiet way its first hour explained, almost without words, what kind of game it intended to be.

Read the full postShow less

Onboarding. It teaches combat beautifully, dodge, switch, chain, in small rooms that feel like a rehearsal, yet it hides its most unusual idea, exploring the Hollows on a grid of TV screens, behind a pace that asks for patience before it has earned any trust. HoYoverse later reworked much of that mode, which tells me players felt the same.

Combat feel. Everything is built around the rhythm of the perfect dodge and the moment an enemy is dazed, so that even a newcomer feels skilled within minutes, and that small taste of mastery, far more than any tutorial text, is what keeps someone playing past the first evening.

Market position. In a crowded field of action RPGs selling ever larger open worlds, it chose style over scale: a compact city, a video store as home base, and a look and sound closer to a music video than to a fantasy epic.

What I took from it, as someone who cuts trailers for a living, is that the first hour of a game is itself a trailer, only one you can play, and it deserves the same care in hook, pace and identity.

Project management

PMP on a 20-hour week

This October I finished my PMP certification and Atlassian's Jira and project planning training, and I did both while working 20 hours a week on trailers and community videos for Paradox Interactive, which taught me the first lesson of project management before the course had even started: time is the one resource no brief ever gives you more of.

Read the full postShow less

Why a video producer studies something that sounds so far from creative work is a fair question, and my answer is simple: every good idea I have seen die did not die because it was bad, it died because nobody wrote down who does what and by when. A sale trailer has a fixed date, a review round with marketing, a version for YouTube and another for TikTok, and if one of those pieces slips, the whole idea arrives late, which in games marketing means it does not arrive at all.

What I actually use every day is far less impressive than the certificate. One board where every request has an owner and a date, one honest look each morning at what is blocked, and a schedule that leaves room for the edit to go wrong, because it always does.

What no certificate can teach is people: how to tell a teammate their cut is not ready yet without losing them, or how to say no to a request politely enough that you are asked again. That part I am still learning, every week.

Producers, which tool do you open every single day before anything else?

Hearts of Iron IV

The best opening I ever made was ten years old

The best opening I ever made for a Hearts of Iron IV video was not mine, it was ten years old, and I found it only after I stopped looking at my own timeline and started looking backwards.

Read the full postShow less

In April we were preparing a short for the Anniversary Sale, and I had everything a sale video usually needs: animated footage from the DLCs, cuts from the official trailers, and gameplay that shows what makes the game what it is. On paper it worked, yet every time I watched the first seconds I had the same uncomfortable feeling that a scroller would not stop for it, because the beginning explained the game instead of making anyone feel something.

So I did something that felt almost like procrastination at the time. I sat down and watched every official Hearts of Iron IV trailer ever released, going back to the announcement of the game a decade ago, hoping that somewhere in that archive there was a tone I had forgotten.

It was in the very first one. That trailer combined real historical footage with a Churchill speech running underneath, and the moment I heard it I knew I had my beginning. I did not take the footage, only the speech, and let it build the tension for the first seconds before the rest of my edit took over, so the voice carried the weight and my images could follow it instead of having to fight for attention on their own.

It became the strongest piece of engagement on the Hearts of Iron IV channel that I have worked on, and it beat the channel's previous record. It also taught me something I did not plan for: the opening was so serious that part of the audience was sure they were watching the announcement of Hearts of Iron V, not a sale. That is a compliment and a warning in the same comment section, because a hook promises something, and the producer's job is to know exactly what it promises before it goes out.

Two things I took away from it. First, when your own ideas run dry, the archive of the brand you work for is often richer than anything new you can invent, and ten-year-old material can feel fresh to people who never saw it. Second, test your first two seconds not by asking whether they look good, but by asking what a stranger will believe they are about to watch.

Game Jam Madrid · Tech Show Madrid

Judging Game Jam Madrid, then walking Tech Show

On Tuesday evening I judged Game Jam Madrid, and the next day I walked the halls of Tech Show at IFEMA MADRID, and somewhere between the two I realised that the most valuable skill in both rooms was the same one, even though one room smelled of pizza and cola and the other of coffee and lanyards.

Read the full postShow less

At the jam, teams had five hours at The Bridge to build a game that runs in a browser, and with credits for Rosebud AI, Meshy and ElevenLabs on the table, making art, 3D models and voices was almost free, so the expensive part was no longer making things but deciding which things deserved to exist at all.

The range of ideas was wonderful: an educational platformer set inside Goya's paintings, a 4D puzzle platformer, a strategy game about Napoleon's invasion of Russia, a town builder where the will of God can send a flood or an earthquake onto your opponent, and, because every jam has one brave soul who ignores the brief, a solo developer who built a smart grocery shopping app.

The team that impressed me most did not invent new rules. They took Coup, the tabletop game of bluffing and influence, set it in the Russian Revolution, and turned it into an online multiplayer game with the social energy of Mafia, Jackbox, Among Us and Liar's Bar, where every player fights for influence and nobody quite trusts anyone. Because the core loop was already proven, they could spend their five hours on the part players feel, the tension around the table, and that is a production decision before it is a design one.

The next day I arrived at Tech Show later than planned, because work comes first, and Tres Cantos, however lovely, is not exactly next door to IFEMA. The day was still worth it: I had good conversations with people from El Corte Inglés and Cegid, stopped by JetBrains and Garaje de ideas | Groupe EDG, and noticed that almost every stand promised the same thing in a slightly different font: more content, faster, with AI. The best conversations were never about the tools, though, but about the people who decide what all that content is for, and how few of them there are compared to what can now be made in an afternoon.

This is where I think production still lives. Tools make content, and they make it quicker every month, but somebody still has to look at a five-hour jam, or a twenty-hour week, or a campaign across three markets, and say this goes in, this waits, and this we drop, early enough that the team can still do the rest well.

Career

From HVAC negotiations to sale trailers

Not so long ago I was sitting across the table from Daikin, Mitsubishi, Samsung and Hitachi, negotiating supplier agreements for an HVAC company in Marbella, and today I make sale trailers and community content for Paradox Interactive games, which on paper looks like a career that took a wrong turn somewhere in the air conditioning aisle.

Read the full postShow less

As Deputy and later Acting Director at Estrella Clima Profesional, I coordinated a multi-million euro development project, negotiated with suppliers far bigger than us, and built the digital side of the company from scratch: the websites, the automated lead generation, the SEO and the campaigns that brought customers through the door.

None of that involved a single game, and yet almost all of it came with me to Paradox, because every trailer for a sale, every piece made for the community and every video for a new TikTok channel is still a production, with people who have different priorities, a deadline that does not care about your mood, a budget of time that is always smaller than the idea, and the uncomfortable duty of saying no to good ideas so that the important ones survive.

What I did have to learn from zero was everything that makes games marketing work: how a strategy community talks, jokes and remembers, how a sale trailer has to turn a scroll into a purchase, and how a short video has to earn every next second of attention, from the first frame to the last.

So I am curious about the other side of the table: if you came into games from another industry, which skill from your old life turned out to be the most useful in the new one? Mine, without a doubt, is negotiation, although I still have not managed to negotiate a longer deadline with the TikTok algorithm XD

Hearts of Iron IV · Process

Four episodes on a 20-hour week

I made four episodes of "I Got Reincarnated as a Field Marshal and Now I'm Stuck on a Beach" on a 20 hour a week contract, and somehow this is the most professional sentence I have written all year.

Read the full postShow less

A part-time week punishes every wasted hour, so I want to show you how those episodes were actually put together, starting with the confession that the whole series grew out of my love for the substories of the Yakuza games, where the story pauses and the hero spends an afternoon on something gloriously ridiculous.

The script came first, and it was built around the El Alamein joke that Montgomery simply cannot stop making, which the community adored so much that in the fourth episode, where I left the joke out entirely, they quoted it in the comments themselves, and that was the moment I understood that a good meme belongs to the audience long before it belongs to its author.

So I would love to hear from anyone who produces on a part-time schedule: how do you budget your time, and what would you change in my process? I am fully aware that a smarter way exists, and I am equally aware that the Hawaiian shirts are staying XD
